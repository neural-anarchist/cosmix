About this model

The trace is a two-dimensional cross-section. The cylindrical option extrudes it into a line focus; the rotationally symmetric option revolves it into a point focus. Both are idealised bounds, and a real bottle lies between them.

The traced cross-section is converted to either a line focus or a point-focus estimate.

Advanced optics

Physical throughput adds Fresnel and liquid absorption losses. Branching traces reflected rays and reports them under Ray fate only. Dispersion is a display effect; reported values use the central index.

Model checks

    Model limits

    This is a geometric-optics and lumped-heating model. Open model limits before interpreting absolute values.

    • 2D ray tracing. Only the meridional plane is traced.
    • Ideal bottle geometry. Uniform wall, exact circular caps.
    • Geometric optics, not wave optics. No diffraction or scattering.
    • Temperature model, not ignition model. One lumped node.
    Show all assumptions
    • Meridional section only. Skew rays that leave the plane of the drawing are not traced.
    • Ideal geometry. Uniform wall thickness and exact circular caps; no neck, shoulder, seam, base, label, or mould line.
    • Geometric optics. No diffraction, interference, or scattering from bubbles, condensation, or surface texture.
    • Single wavelength. Reported values use one refractive index. The solar spectrum and wavelength-dependent absorption are not modelled.
    • Finite Sun. The 0.53° solar disc blurs the focus by about fΘ, combined in quadrature with the traced spread.
    • Idealised third dimension. Cylindrical and revolved concentrations are bounds on one 2D trace; a real bottle lies between them.
    • Separate absorptivity and emissivity. Solar absorption and thermal re-emission occupy different wavelength bands, so they are independent parameters.
    • Lumped thermal node. No lateral conduction, conduction into the support, evaporative cooling, pyrolysis, or ignition criterion.
    • Linearised convection. One coefficient h stands in for free convection.
    • Ray-density radiometry. Irradiance is estimated in 0.5 mm cells from ray spacing. Caustic peaks are resolution-limited.
    • Branching is capped and separate. Reflected sub-rays are traced to a fixed depth and reported apart from the headline values.

    Treat trends as the useful output and absolute values as order-of-magnitude estimates.

    04 — Theory

    Model and assumptions

    The model resolves refraction at each interface, estimates irradiance from ray density, and uses a lumped thermal balance for the target.

    Ray tracing through a thick bottle

    Each ray is refracted at every interface with the vector form of Snell's law, using the local surface normal. A full bottle gives four refractions in sequence: air, wall, liquid, wall, air. A ray beyond the critical angle reflects internally instead.

    $$n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$$

    Because the normal rotates along a curved cap, the bending is not constant across the aperture. That is the source of aberration.

    A bottle is not a thin lens. With a 120 mm body, a ray travels more than a hundred millimetres between the two curved surfaces, at a height that changes along the way. Each interface therefore gets its own local normal, and no focal length is assumed. The paraxial thick-lens formula appears in the derivation for comparison only.

    Show derivation

    A tracer works with a unit direction $\mathbf{d}$ and unit normal $\mathbf{n}$, not angles. The wave-vector component parallel to the interface is continuous, so with $\eta = n_1/n_2$:

    $$n_1\,\mathbf{d}_\parallel = n_2\,\mathbf{t}_\parallel \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{t}_\parallel = \eta\,\mathbf{d}_\parallel$$

    Requiring $|\mathbf{t}| = 1$ fixes the normal component:

    $$\mathbf{t} = \eta\,\mathbf{d} + (\eta\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2)\,\mathbf{n}, \qquad \cos\theta_1 = -\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}, \qquad \cos\theta_2 = \sqrt{1-\eta^2(1-\cos^2\theta_1)}$$

    When the square root is imaginary there is no transmitted ray:

    $$\sin\theta_1 > n_2/n_1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{d} - 2(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n})\mathbf{n}$$

    On a circular arc of centre $\mathbf{C}$ and radius $R$, the outward normal at $\mathbf{P}$ is

    $$\mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{C}}{R}$$

    A ray entering parallel to the axis at height $y$ meets the front cap with $\sin\theta_1 = y/R$.

    For comparison, the paraxial thick-lens result for one medium of index $n$ between surfaces of radii $R_1$ and $R_2$, separated by an axial thickness $d$, is

    $$\frac{1}{f} = (n-1)\left[\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{(n-1)\,d}{n\,R_1 R_2}\right]$$

    with back focal distance $f\left[1 - \dfrac{(n-1)d}{nR_1}\right]$ from the rear vertex. The thickness term can shift the focus by tens of percent for a bottle. The traced paraxial focus also includes the wall menisci that this formula omits.

    Why the focus spreads out

    Rays entering at different heights cross the axis at different distances. This spherical aberration turns the focus into a caustic, the bright arrowhead in the ray diagram. The narrowest spot sits slightly inside the paraxial focus, and narrowing the beam removes most of the spread.

    $$\mathrm{LSA} = z_{\text{marginal}} - z_{\text{paraxial}}$$

    Aberration depends strongly on aperture. Narrow the beam and the spread nearly vanishes; open it to the full bottle diameter and the outer rays can cross at less than half the paraxial distance. Chromatic aberration, from the wavelength dependence of the index, is only about 2% for water and is shown as an optional display.

    Show derivation

    Near the axis the bending grows in proportion to $\sin\theta_1$. Further out it falls behind, so outer rays are bent relatively too hard and cross the axis early. Because the crossing point varies smoothly with height, the envelope of the exit rays is a cusped caustic rather than a point, and the narrowest spot is the circle of least confusion.

    Chromatic aberration is smaller. For water, $n$ falls from about 1.337 in the blue to about 1.331 in the red. Since the focal distance scales roughly as $1/(n-1)$,

    $$\frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx -\frac{\Delta n}{n-1} \approx -\frac{0.006}{0.333} \approx 1.8\%$$

    The default model is monochromatic. The dispersion toggle traces three bundles at $n \pm \delta$ for display only; reported values use the central index.

    Turning a 2D trace into irradiance

    The Sun is not a point, so any focus is blurred by about $f\,\Theta$, where $\Theta \approx 0.53^\circ$. This blur combines with the traced spread in quadrature. The trace is a section, so the third dimension must be chosen. Extruding it gives a line focus with first-order gain; revolving it gives a point focus with second-order gain.

    $$w = \sqrt{w_{\text{traced}}^2 + (f\,\Theta)^2}$$ $$C_{\text{cylindrical}} \approx \frac{D_{\text{beam}}}{w}, \qquad C_{\text{revolved}} \approx \left(\frac{D_{\text{beam}}}{w}\right)^{2}$$

    A geometry that concentrates 25× as a cylinder concentrates about 600× when revolved. Switching modes changes only the radiometric bookkeeping, not the traced rays. The solar blur changes the reported spot width and everything derived from it, but not the plotted irradiance profile.

    Physical throughput adds two losses: unpolarised Fresnel reflection at each interface, and optional Beer–Lambert absorption in the liquid. Together they cost roughly 8–10% near normal incidence and grow sharply toward the rim. Optional branching traces the reflected light and reports it separately.

    Show derivation

    The traced beam is parallel, but a perfect lens images the solar disc, not a point. The two contributions are independent, so they add in quadrature. The blur applies only to focused dimensions: both transverse directions in revolved mode, and the in-plane direction only in cylindrical mode.

    Revolve the profile about the axis and a ray at height $y$ stands for an annulus, so it is weighted by its circumference and the focus is a point. Extrude it by a width $W$ and every ray carries equal power. Both are idealised bounds: one assumes perfect on-axis symmetry, the other a straight cylinder lit exactly broadside.

    To model the broadside geometry, set the body length to zero and both cap radii to $D/2$. The profile becomes the circular cross-section of the barrel, and cylindrical mode reads it as a line focus.

    Each ray carries power

    $$P_{\text{ray}} = E_0 \cdot \Delta y \cdot W \cdot \tau$$

    in the extruded model, where $\tau$ is the accumulated Fresnel transmittance. In the revolved model the same ray carries the power of its annulus, proportional to $|y|\,\Delta y$. At the target, each ray is spread over a tube as wide as its spacing to its neighbours, and the irradiance in a cell is the power in it divided by its area. Bunching raises the profile; fanning out flattens it to 1×. No smoothing kernel is involved, and peaks at a caustic depend on the cell size.

    The unpolarised Fresnel transmittance averages the two polarisations:

    $$R_s = \left[\frac{n_1\cos\theta_1 - n_2\cos\theta_2}{n_1\cos\theta_1 + n_2\cos\theta_2}\right]^2, \qquad R_p = \left[\frac{n_1\cos\theta_2 - n_2\cos\theta_1}{n_1\cos\theta_2 + n_2\cos\theta_1}\right]^2$$ $$\tau = \prod_{\text{interfaces}} \left[1 - \tfrac12(R_s+R_p)\right] \cdot e^{-\mu L}$$

    The factor $e^{-\mu L}$ is Beer–Lambert absorption over the path length $L$ spent in the liquid. With physical throughput off, $\tau \equiv 1$. Branching traces the reflected fraction $1-\tau$ as recursive sub-rays and reports the recovered power under Ray fate, separately from the headline values.

    Heating model

    The target is one lumped node. Absorbed sunlight heats it; convection and thermal radiation cool it. Every term scales with the patch area, so within this model the steady temperature depends on irradiance rather than on total collected power.

    $$C\,\frac{dT}{dt} = \alpha_{\text{solar}}\,E_{\text{target}} - h(T-T_0) - \varepsilon_{\text{thermal}}\,\sigma\,(T^4-T_0^4)$$

    Solar absorptivity and thermal emissivity are separate parameters because sunlight and a warm target radiate in different wavelength bands. The radiative term matters: at 750 K a surface with $\varepsilon_{\text{thermal}} = 0.9$ radiates about 18 kW m⁻².

    The area cancellation is a statement about this one equation, not about real apparatus. Real targets also conduct heat sideways, real convection depends on target size, and a larger aperture forms a larger image of the Sun. Heat capacity and the loss coefficient are per unit area, and both emissivity and absorptivity default to 0.9, a plausible value for an ordinary dark surface.

    Show derivation

    The absorbed power over a patch of area $A$ uses the absorptivity for sunlight:

    $$P_{\text{abs}} = \alpha_{\text{solar}} \cdot E_{\text{target}} \cdot A$$

    With areal heat capacity $C$ [J m⁻² K⁻¹] and loss coefficient $h$ [W m⁻² K⁻¹], the balance for the patch is

    $$C\,A\,\frac{dT}{dt} = \alpha_{\text{solar}}\,E_{\text{target}}\,A - hA(T-T_0) - \varepsilon_{\text{thermal}}\,\sigma A\,(T^4-T_0^4)$$

    Dividing by $A$ gives the equation above.

    Kirchhoff's law equates spectral absorptivity and emissivity at the same wavelength, direction, and polarisation, for a surface in local thermal equilibrium. Sunlight (about 5778 K, peaking near 500 nm) and a target's own emission (mid-infrared) occupy different bands, so $\alpha(\lambda)$ and $\varepsilon(\lambda)$ can differ strongly. A selective solar absorber is built to have high $\alpha_{\text{solar}}$ and low $\varepsilon_{\text{thermal}}$.

    What the model leaves out

    The model reports a temperature, not ignition. It omits lateral conduction, evaporative cooling, pyrolysis kinetics, the solar spectrum, and real-bottle defects such as seams, necks, and labels. The Sun is modelled as a uniform disc, with no limb darkening, haze, or cloud.

    Read the trends as the useful output: which geometry concentrates more, how the focus moves with index, how quickly aberration erodes the gain. Read absolute temperatures as order-of-magnitude estimates. Scorching a dark surface needs a few hundred degrees; sustained ignition of wood or paper is a chemical-kinetic threshold, not a temperature read off a graph.
