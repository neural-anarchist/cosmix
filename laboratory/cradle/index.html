Magnetic Pendulum Cradle

A row of equal-length pendulums, each carrying a magnet. Every dipole points the same way in space and stays that way as the pendulums swing, so the coupling comes entirely from how the bobs move relative to one another — and one angle controls whether they attract, repel, or ignore each other completely.

Section two

Spring–Mass Analog

We linearize the pendulum equations about the resting position to obtain a system of coupled linear oscillators, which can be represented as masses and springs along a horizontal track. Each pendulum's angular displacement becomes a linear one, $x_i = L\,\eta_i$; gravity becomes a spring to ground and each magnetic interaction becomes a spring between neighbours. Because every pendulum now shares one length, this mapping is exact rather than approximate.

Driven directly from the pendulum state above, so the two views stay exactly in step. Vertical springs are the gravitational restoring force; horizontal springs are the magnetic coupling — drawn in rust when G(α) is negative, since the coupling spring is then inverted.

Section four

Normal Modes & Theory

From Newton's laws for each individual pendulum to a single matrix eigenvalue problem, and then to a closed-form answer.

1 · Geometry and the dipole interaction

Pendulum $i$ has its pivot at $x = (i-1)d$ and length $L$, so its bob sits at

$$\mathbf{r}_i = \big((i-1)d + L\sin\theta_i,\; -L\cos\theta_i\big)$$

Every bob carries the same dipole direction $\hat{\mathbf{s}} = (\cos\alpha,\ \sin\alpha)$, fixed in space — the magnets do not turn as the rods swing. With $\mathbf{m}_i = \mu_i\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ and $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_i$, the dipole–dipole energy is

$$U_{ij} = \frac{\mu_0\mu_i\mu_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{1 - 3(\hat{\mathbf{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})^2}{r^3}$$

The simulation evaluates this exactly and differentiates it analytically. Near the resting position the bobs sit at the same height, so $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{s}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \cos\alpha$, giving

$$\begin{aligned} U_{ij} &\;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\mu_0\mu_i\mu_j}{4\pi}\,\frac{G(\alpha)}{u_{ij}^{3}} \\[4pt] G(\alpha) &= 1 - 3\cos^2\alpha \\[2pt] u_{ij} &= (j-i)\,d + L\,(\theta_j-\theta_i) \end{aligned}$$

$G(\alpha)$ is the whole story of the coupling. At $\alpha = 90^\circ$ — every north pole straight up, the default here — $G = +1$: the magnets sit side-by-side and repel. At $\alpha = 0^\circ$, $G = -2$: they lie head-to-tail and attract. At $\alpha = \arccos(1/\sqrt3) \approx 54.74^\circ$, $G = 0$ exactly and the pendulums decouple no matter how strong the magnets are.

Finite magnets. A point dipole diverges as two bobs meet, and long before that the formula stops describing a real magnet of finite size. The simulation therefore evaluates the interaction at $q = \sqrt{r^2+\varepsilon^2}$ with $\varepsilon = 0.03\,d$ standing in for the magnet's own extent. Because $q$ replaces $r$ inside the potential, and the torques are its exact gradient, energy is still conserved to machine precision. At normal separations the change is a fraction of a percent, and the decoupling at the magic angle survives to about one part in ten thousand.

2 · Newton's second law for each pendulum

Each bob is a point mass on a massless rod, so pendulum $i$ has moment of inertia $I_i = m_i L^2$ about its pivot. Summing torques — gravity, linear damping, and one term per magnetic partner — gives $N$ simultaneous equations:

$$m_i L^2\,\ddot\theta_i \;=\; -m_i g L\sin\theta_i \;-\; b\,m_i L^2\,\dot\theta_i \;+\; \sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}$$ $$\tau_{ij} = -\,\partial U_{ij}/\partial\theta_i$$

Written out in full for $N = 3$, with $\tau_{ij} = -\tau_{ji}$ by Newton's third law:

$$\begin{aligned} m_1 L^2\ddot\theta_1 &= -m_1 gL\sin\theta_1 - b\,m_1L^2\dot\theta_1 + \tau_{12} + \tau_{13}\\[2pt] m_2 L^2\ddot\theta_2 &= -m_2 gL\sin\theta_2 - b\,m_2L^2\dot\theta_2 - \tau_{12} + \tau_{23}\\[2pt] m_3 L^2\ddot\theta_3 &= -m_3 gL\sin\theta_3 - b\,m_3L^2\dot\theta_3 - \tau_{13} - \tau_{23} \end{aligned}$$

Note that every pendulum couples to every other, not just to its neighbour. Because $\kappa \propto 1/d^5$, however, the next-nearest term is $2^5 = 32$ times weaker than the adjacent one.

3 · The resting position

Expanding $U_{ij}$ in the small displacement $\delta = L(\theta_j-\theta_i)$,

$$U_{ij} \approx \frac{CG}{D^3}\left[\,1 - \frac{3\delta}{D} + \frac{6\delta^2}{D^2}\,\right]$$ $$C = \frac{\mu_0\mu_i\mu_j}{4\pi}, \qquad D = (j-i)\,d$$

The linear term does not vanish: the magnets exert a net pull (or push) even when the rods hang vertically, so $\theta = 0$ is not an equilibrium. The true resting position $\boldsymbol{\theta}^\ast$ solves

$$-m_i g L\sin\theta^\ast_i + \sum_{j\neq i}\tau_{ij}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\ast) = 0 \quad\text{for all } i$$

which we solve by damped Newton–Raphson. Everything that follows is expanded about $\boldsymbol{\theta}^\ast$, writing $\eta_i = \theta_i - \theta^\ast_i$.

4 · Linearization, and the conversion to matrices

The quadratic term above is the coupling. Matching it to a difference-form potential $\tfrac12\kappa_{ij}(\theta_i-\theta_j)^2$ gives

$$\boxed{\;\kappa_{ij} \;=\; \frac{12\,\mu_0\,\mu_i\mu_j\,G(\alpha)\,L^2}{4\pi\,u_{ij}^{\ast\,5}}\;}$$

Note $u^\ast_{ij}$, the separation at the resting position — not the nominal $(j-i)d$. Because $\kappa \propto 1/u^5$, the few-percent gap change caused by the magnets themselves moves the coupling by tens of percent, so using the nominal spacing here would be a real error rather than a rounding one.

Substituting $\sin\theta_i \to \theta^\ast_i + \eta_i$ and keeping first order, each equation becomes

$$m_i L^2\ddot\eta_i = -\Big(m_i g L\cos\theta^\ast_i + \sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{ij}\Big)\eta_i + \sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{ij}\,\eta_j - b\,m_iL^2\dot\eta_i$$

Collecting the $N$ equations into one and dropping damping, the system is exactly $\mathbf{M}\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta} + \mathbf{K}\boldsymbol\eta = \mathbf{0}$. For $N = 3$ that reads

$$\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} m_1L^2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & m_2L^2 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & m_3L^2\end{pmatrix}$$ $$\mathbf{K} = \begin{pmatrix} K_{11} & -\kappa_{12} & -\kappa_{13}\\ -\kappa_{12} & K_{22} & -\kappa_{23}\\ -\kappa_{13} & -\kappa_{23} & K_{33} \end{pmatrix}$$ $$\begin{aligned} K_{11} &= m_1gL\cos\theta^\ast_1 + \kappa_{12} + \kappa_{13} + \sigma_1\\ K_{22} &= m_2gL\cos\theta^\ast_2 + \kappa_{12} + \kappa_{23} + \sigma_2\\ K_{33} &= m_3gL\cos\theta^\ast_3 + \kappa_{13} + \kappa_{23} + \sigma_3 \end{aligned}$$

In general

$$\begin{aligned} M_{ij} &= m_i L^2\,\delta_{ij} \\[3pt] K_{ii} &= m_i g L\cos\theta^\ast_i + \sum_{j\neq i}\kappa_{ij} \;+\; \sigma_i \\[3pt] K_{ij} &= -\kappa_{ij} \qquad (i\neq j) \end{aligned}$$

So $\mathbf{K}$ is a diagonal gravity term plus the graph Laplacian of the magnetic couplings. That structure is the reason the results below look the way they do.

The extra diagonal piece $\sigma_i$ appears only when the dipoles are oblique. A tilted dipole direction singles out a handedness, breaking the left–right mirror symmetry of the row, so the two members of a pair are stiffened by equal and opposite amounts:

$$\sigma_i = \sum_{j\neq i}\operatorname{sgn}(j-i)\,\frac{3\,\mu_0\,\mu_i\mu_j\,L\,\sin 2\alpha}{4\pi\,u_{ij}^{\ast\,4}}$$

It couples nothing — it only shifts each pendulum's own restoring torque — and it vanishes identically at $\alpha = 0^\circ$ and $\alpha = 90^\circ$, where $\sin 2\alpha = 0$. That is why the closed form of the next section is exact at those two orientations and approximate between them.

5 · The eigenvalue problem

Substituting $\boldsymbol\eta(t) = \mathbf{v}\,e^{i\omega t}$ turns the differential equation into the generalized eigenvalue problem

$$\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\,\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v},\qquad \lambda = \omega^2$$

Since $\mathbf{M}$ is diagonal and positive definite we avoid the non-symmetric $\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{K}$ by symmetrizing: with $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{M}^{-1/2}$, the matrix $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{S}$ is symmetric and shares the eigenvalues. A Jacobi rotation sweep diagonalizes it, and the eigenvectors map back as $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{S}\mathbf{u}$, arriving $\mathbf{M}$-orthonormal:

$$\mathbf{v}_k^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}_l = \delta_{kl}$$

A normal mode is a motion in which every pendulum oscillates at the same frequency with a fixed amplitude ratio — the cradle keeps its shape and only breathes. Any initial condition is a superposition of them, and the beating you see above is two modes of slightly different frequency drifting in and out of step.

6 · Closed-form solution for the uniform chain

When every pendulum is identical and we keep only nearest-neighbour coupling, $\mathbf{K} = mgL\,\mathbf{I} + \kappa\mathbf{L}_{\text{path}}$, and the path-graph Laplacian has a known spectrum. The result is fully analytic:

$$\boxed{\;\omega_k^2 \;=\; \frac{g}{L} \;+\; \frac{4\kappa}{mL^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{k\pi}{2N}\right), \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,N-1\;}$$ $$v_k[i] \;=\; \cos\!\left(\frac{k\pi\,(i+\tfrac12)}{N}\right)$$

Two consequences are worth stating plainly. First, $k=0$ gives $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ exactly: the mode is uniform, a Laplacian annihilates uniform vectors, and the magnets cannot touch it. Second, the sign of $\kappa$ decides the ordering — with $G(\alpha) < 0$ the coupling is negative and the anti-phase mode is the soft one, the reverse of the familiar spring-coupled case.

Where the closed form stops being exact. It assumes every pendulum identical and only nearest-neighbour coupling.
